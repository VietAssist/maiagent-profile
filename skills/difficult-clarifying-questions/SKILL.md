# SKILL: Asking clarifying questions without seeming incompetent

## When to use this skill
When the user needs to ask a client or manager for more information but is worried about looking confused, unprepared, or incompetent.

## Core principle
Asking good questions is a sign of intelligence and professionalism in Western work culture. Making assumptions and getting it wrong is far worse than asking upfront.

## The reframe for the user
In Vietnamese work culture, asking questions can feel like admitting you don't know something — which risks losing face. In Western culture, NOT asking and then delivering the wrong thing is what loses trust.

## Frameworks for asking clarifying questions

### Before starting a project
"Before I dive in, I have a few quick questions to make sure I deliver exactly what you need:"
Then list 3–5 specific questions. Numbered. Short.

### When a brief is unclear
"I want to make sure I'm aligned before I start — could you help me understand [specific unclear point]?"

### When instructions change mid-project
"I want to make sure I'm working from the latest direction — am I right in understanding that we're now [new understanding]? Just want to confirm before I move forward."

### When you don't understand something on a call
"Just to make sure I've got this right — are you saying [restate what you heard]? I want to make sure I don't miss anything."

## Phrases that soften the ask without being weak
- "Quick question before I proceed..."
- "Just want to confirm my understanding..."
- "To make sure I'm on the right track..."
- "I want to get this right, so..."

## What NOT to say
- "Sorry for the stupid question but..." → removes the apology entirely
- Vague questions: "What do you want?" → be specific about what you need to know

## Output format
Help the user draft their specific clarifying questions. Ask: what do they not understand, and who are they asking?
